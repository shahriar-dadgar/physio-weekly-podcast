Physio Weekly for Sabaa: November 1, 2026

Sunday, 1 November 2026 · 9 min

Welcome to this week's research roundup for Sabaa. We have a substantial batch of new evidence to cover, spanning pharmacology, neurorehabilitation, orthopedics, and digital health. Let's start with the scientific literature.

A new Cochrane systematic review has tackled a long-standing question in stroke rehabilitation: whether levodopa or dopamine agonists can enhance motor recovery when combined with therapy. The review searched through November 2025 for randomized trials comparing these dopaminergic agents against control groups. This is a high-priority area because these drugs are sometimes used off-label in interdisciplinary stroke programs. However, the published abstract is truncated; it details the methodology and inclusion criteria but does not report the pooled effect estimates, GRADE certainty ratings, or the review's final conclusions. We'll need the full text to see whether the highest level of evidence supports this pharmacological adjunct.

Staying in neurorehabilitation, the Italian Neuropsychological Society has published clinical practice guidelines for speech and language therapy in both post-stroke aphasia and primary progressive aphasia. A multidisciplinary panel used systematic review methodology to address four specific questions not fully covered by existing international guidelines: the effectiveness of therapy delivered by specialists versus non-specialized personnel; therapy in the chronic phase versus no treatment; and whether adding non-invasive neuromodulation to standard therapy provides additional benefit for either condition. The abstract outlines the rigorous development process and the scope of the questions, but it does not state the actual recommendations or their strength. For clinicians managing aphasia across both vascular and neurodegenerative populations, the full guideline will be an important reference.

Moving to geriatrics and falls prevention, a network meta-analysis published in JMIR Aging has updated the evidence on digital exercise interventions for balance in older adults. The analysis includes sixty randomized trials encompassing nearly three thousand five hundred participants with a mean age of seventy-three. It compares five technology categories—exergaming with balance platforms, exergaming with motion capture, serious games, wearables, and telerehabilitation—against conventional exercise and no treatment. The authors note that a previous 2023 network meta-analysis found only exergaming with motion capture to be superior to no treatment. This update expands the evidence base, but the abstract provided is truncated; specific comparative effectiveness estimates, treatment rankings, and GRADE certainty ratings for each comparison are not available in the supplied text.

In orthopedics, a systematic review and meta-analysis of fifteen randomized trials involving over a thousand patients evaluated acupuncture and electroacupuncture for postoperative management after total knee arthroplasty in older adults. The findings are statistically significant: acupuncture was associated with reduced postoperative pain, a standardized mean difference of minus one point zero eight on the visual analog scale, alongside improved functional scores on the WOMAC and Hospital for Special Surgery measures, increased range of motion, and reduced analgesic consumption. This supports acupuncture as an effective adjunct for multimodal perioperative protocols. The abstract does not detail risk of bias assessments, heterogeneity statistics, or GRADE certainty, so those appraisal steps remain for the full paper.

For ACL rehabilitation, a network meta-analysis of thirty-two trials and nearly fifteen hundred participants provides a hierarchy of motor-control interventions. Published in Complementary Therapies in Medicine, the analysis used CINeMA and GRADE to rate confidence. Proprioceptive training improved joint position sense versus usual rehabilitation with a standardized mean difference of one point zero two, rated at moderate confidence. Jump-landing control training showed a favorable effect on dynamic balance, SMD one point one nine, though confidence in that estimate was rated low. Other biomechanical comparisons were either pooled from at least two studies or left unpooled when only a single trial was available. This gives physiotherapists a graded evidence base for selecting specific neuromuscular exercises to address persistent deficits after reconstruction.

Two major pragmatic trials have published design papers with results still pending. In the American Journal of Sports Medicine, a multicenter, double-blind RCT randomized two hundred forty patients with Kellgren-Lawrence grade one or two knee osteoarthritis to either a standardized clinical pathway for platelet-rich plasma therapy—including standardized patient selection, quality-controlled leukocyte-poor PRP, ultrasound-guided injection, staged rehabilitation, and systematic follow-up—or conventional physician-directed PRP care. The twelve-month primary outcomes are VAS pain and WOMAC, but the abstract does not report results or between-group differences.

Similarly, the FIRST trial, published in the BMJ, recruited four hundred thirty patients across twenty-six UK hospitals to compare three common splinting regimens for zone one and two flexor tendon repairs: a forearm-based long splint, the Manchester short splint, and a relative motion flexion mini-splint. The primary outcome is the mean post-randomization PRWHE score. The pragmatic design enhances generalizability to routine hand therapy practice, though participants could not be masked. Again, results are not in the abstract.

Finally, a systematic review and meta-analysis in JMIR mHealth and uHealth examined wrist-worn wearable-guided cardiac rehabilitation delivered through fully remote or hybrid programs. The review focused on peak VO2, six-minute walk distance, and daily steps as primary outcomes. While a random-effects meta-analysis was conducted, the abstract is truncated and does not provide pooled effect estimates, heterogeneity measures, or certainty of evidence. Studies focused on heart failure or using wearables solely for outcome measurement were excluded. This evidence will be relevant as hybrid cardiac rehab models continue to expand.

That brings us to technology and innovation, where three developments are worth tracking.

First, a study reported in the European Medical Journal provides high-level clinical evidence that telerehabilitation for multiple sclerosis achieves outcomes comparable to conventional face-to-face delivery. For physiotherapists, this is a significant data point: it supports the confident implementation or expansion of remote programs for MS patients to improve access, adherence, and continuity of care without compromising effectiveness. The evidence status is a peer-reviewed clinical trial, so this moves the conversation beyond feasibility into comparative effectiveness.

Second, reporting from the South China Morning Post indicates that China is advancing low-intensity focused ultrasound therapy into clinical application for disorders of consciousness following brain injury. This represents a progression from research into clinical translation for a non-invasive neuromodulation approach. If validated, it could expand the neurorehabilitation toolkit for patients with severe brain injury. However, this is currently independent technology reporting on clinical development, not a peer-reviewed efficacy study. Physiotherapists and neurorehabilitation teams should watch for published trial data before integrating this into practice pathways.

Third, materials science is pushing wearable sensors forward. Researchers have developed a printable hydrogel capable of fabricating low-cost, multifunctional wearable sensors that can simultaneously track strain, temperature, and electrophysiological signals. Reported in Medical Product Outsourcing, this technology could enable customizable, affordable sensors for real-time movement analysis, muscle activity monitoring, and remote patient management. The potential for digital therapeutics and enhanced telerehabilitation is clear, but the evidence status remains early-stage technology reporting. Clinical validation in rehabilitation populations will be the necessary next step.

To close: this week's literature gives us a mix of actionable findings—particularly the acupuncture meta-analysis for TKA and the ACL motor-control hierarchy—alongside several high-profile trials where we await results. The technology landscape continues to move from feasibility toward comparative evidence, notably in MS telerehabilitation. As always, the full texts will fill in the critical details that abstracts leave out. Thanks for listening, and I'll see you next week.
