Physio Weekly for Sabaa: November 23, 2026

Monday, 23 November 2026 · 7 min

Welcome to Physio Weekly for Sabaa, your weekly look at the research and technology shaping rehabilitation. I'm your host, and this week we've got a solid run of trials and reviews, then a few technology developments worth watching. Let's get into it.

We start at the shoulder. A network meta-analysis in Lasers in Medical Science pooled nineteen randomized trials and just under a thousand patients to ask whether adding laser therapy to conventional physiotherapy helps in subacromial impingement. On pain, the signal was cautiously positive: both high-intensity and low-level laser reduced pain scores compared with conventional physiotherapy alone, and neither laser beat the other. Function was different. On the SPADI there was no clear benefit, with certainty ranging from low to moderate for pain and very low to low for function. So laser may ease pain as an adjunct, but there's no good evidence yet that it restores shoulder function.

Staying with recovery, a systematic review in Disability and Rehabilitation examined self-guided home-based rehabilitation for upper limb recovery after stroke. Across fourteen articles, with eight cohorts in the meta-analysis, self-guided programs showed no significant advantage over other approaches; the pooled estimate leaned only slightly in their favour. The review excluded studies using devices, telerehabilitation, or direct therapist supervision, so this speaks to unguided practice specifically. Self-guided work can be a reasonable option, but the evidence doesn't make it superior.

To a combined neurorehabilitation approach. A meta-analysis in Neuroscience pooled thirty trials and more than three thousand patients, comparing acupuncture plus repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation against the stimulation alone for hemiparesis after ischemic stroke. The combination came out ahead on the Fugl-Meyer Assessment, with a total-score difference of about seven points and a larger one on the upper-extremity subscale. Two caveats: there was moderate heterogeneity in the total-score analysis, and this is an adjunctive intervention that requires access to acupuncture, so it sits outside physiotherapy-only care. For services that can offer it, the combination may add motor benefit.

A lower-tech idea next. A systematic review in Disability and Rehabilitation looked at action observation therapy in dementia and cognitive impairment, drawing on nine randomized studies and three hundred and forty-eight patients. Reported effects were sizeable across cognition, activities of daily living, naming in aphasia, gait velocity, and Timed Up-and-Go. That breadth appeals for a low-cost, non-invasive approach, but the sample is small and the certainty of evidence wasn't stated, so treat these as promising rather than settled.

In multiple sclerosis, a double-blinded randomized trial in Clinical Rehabilitation tested adding fatigue-focused cognitive behavioural therapy to supervised Pilates-based core stability training for balance and walking. Ninety participants completed it. Both groups improved significantly, but the group with the psychological add-on reported greater gains. It was a single-centre trial over eight weeks, and the abstract doesn't give full between-group effect sizes, so the direction is favourable while the size of the benefit stays unclear.

Now a null result worth sitting with. A multicentre, assessor-blinded trial in the European Respiratory Journal asked whether starting home-based pulmonary rehabilitation early after a COPD hospitalisation reduced readmissions. Among a hundred and fifty-seven participants, all-cause non-elective hospitalisation at twelve months was no different from usual care. That's a useful corrective, because early home-based pulmonary rehab is often promoted as a way to cut readmissions, and this trial doesn't support that. The abstract doesn't report exercise capacity or quality of life, so it's a specific finding about hospitalisation, not a verdict on the whole programme.

Back to surgery, and to timing. A network meta-analysis in Cancer Nursing compared early mobilization windows after thoracoscopic lung cancer surgery across seventeen studies and more than two thousand patients. The eight-to-twelve-hour window ranked highest for shortening chest tube duration and reducing length of stay. It's a useful signal for post-op pathways, but network rankings are indirect and certainty wasn't reported, so a first-place ranking isn't the same as proven superiority.

We close the science with a core intervention. A systematic review in the International Journal of Rehabilitation Research, using GRADE, examined aerobic training after stroke for walking endurance, gait speed, and balance. On the Six-Minute Walk Test, the pooled randomized evidence showed no significant improvement, a mean difference of under ten metres across nine studies and six hundred and eighty-eight participants. The review included some non-randomized and pre-post studies, and certainty isn't detailed in the abstract. So we shouldn't assume aerobic training reliably boosts walking endurance after stroke, and the effects on speed and balance need a closer read of the full paper.

Let's shift to technology, where the evidence bar is a little different. First, a soft wearable shoulder robot was reported to match physical therapists at delivering stretching to older adults. It's a research comparison rather than a product on the market, and it's striking because it puts a device head-to-head with a therapist on a defined task. If a soft, wearable system can hold its own there, it hints at scalable, consistent assisted therapy and some relief for therapist workload. The open questions are the practical ones: supervision, safety, and where human judgement adds value beyond a standardized movement. We only have the headline and summary here, so the study design and sample size aren't something we can vouch for yet.

Next, a regulatory step. Dynamic Solution is reported to be seeking FDA approval for its X-Hand rehabilitation robot hand, with an eye on the North American market. To be clear, this is a company announcing that it's seeking clearance, not confirmation that approval has been granted. A dedicated robotic hand moving through the FDA pathway does signal that upper-limb rehabilitation robotics are expanding, which could eventually add options for hand and grasp work. But safety, efficacy, and indications aren't independently established at this stage, so it's one to watch rather than one to adopt.

And finally, virtual reality assisted cycling was reported to show feasibility and safety in intensive care. That's an early-stage result, but an important kind, because before VR-augmented exercise can be used for early mobilization in critically ill patients, where immobility and deconditioning are major concerns, someone has to show it's feasible and safe first. If it holds up, VR could make in-bed or bedside activity more tolerable and engaging. As with the shoulder robot, this comes from a headline and summary, so we don't yet know the study size or design.

That's our week. A lot of these studies point the same way: promising in places, but rarely a green light to change practice on their own. Thanks for listening to Physio Weekly for Sabaa, and I'll see you next week.
